Why we ship Whisper Tiny inside the installer (and what that means for privacy)

Most local-first apps make you download the model on first launch. We bundled the smallest one. Here's why a 75 MB add to the installer is the privacy choice - and what changes when you upgrade.

By the OpenVoice maintainers June 14, 2026 8 min read
TL;DR
  • Whisper Tiny (~75 MB, GGML quantized) ships embedded in the OpenVoice installer.
  • Reason 1 - privacy: most "local-first" apps still talk to a model-download CDN on first launch. Bundling means dictation works before the network is ever touched.
  • Reason 2 - air-gapped installs: a sealed laptop can install and dictate without ever phoning home.
  • Tiny is genuinely useful for short-form dictation. Larger models (Small, Medium, Large) download on demand via the auto-provisioner when you want better accuracy.

The OpenVoice installer is ~85 MB on Windows. The empty-app Tauri binary is ~10 MB. The other 75 MB is one file: ggml-tiny-q5_1.bin, the smallest production Whisper variant, quantized to 5-bit precision. That file lives in src-tauri/resources/models/ in the source tree and gets stamped into every installer we ship.

This is unusual. Most local-first apps in this category - MacWhisper, the various Whisper-frontend forks, LM Studio for audio - make you download a model on first launch. The download is usually fast, the UX is usually fine, the model files are usually too big to bundle (Medium is 1.5 GB, Large is 3 GB).

Tiny is small enough to bundle. We bundle it deliberately. This post explains why.

The first-run privacy problem

Here's the lifecycle of "first-launch" for a typical local-first dictation app:

  1. User downloads installer from a website.
  2. User runs installer. Installer puts the binary on disk.
  3. User launches the app.
  4. App opens, shows a "downloading model..." progress bar.
  5. App's process makes an HTTP GET request to a CDN - usually Hugging Face, sometimes a vendor S3 bucket.
  6. ~500 MB later, the app is ready to use.

Steps 4 and 5 are the privacy hole. The user installed a "local" app, but the app's first action was to phone a third-party server and identify itself as a fresh install. That's a fingerprintable event. The CDN's access log now records "this IP, this user-agent, this app version, this model file requested, this exact timestamp." Multiply by every user, and the CDN operator has a real-time install-count dashboard for an app they didn't ship.

For a SaaS app, that's expected. For a local-first app, it's a contradiction. If the architecture says "your audio never leaves your machine," the install path probably shouldn't either.

What "bundled" buys you

OpenVoice ships with Tiny embedded. The first time you launch the app, it doesn't make a network call. It probes your hardware locally, decides whether Tiny is acceptable for your tier, and starts a recording loop with whatever it has on disk. No CDN log, no fingerprintable install event, no "downloading..." screen.

Specifically:

  • You can install OpenVoice on a machine that's never seen the internet, and dictate. See the air-gapped install post for the recipe.
  • You can verify that no network call happens on first-run with a packet capture (Wireshark, tcpdump, your firewall logs). On a typical first-launch the only outbound traffic is the system's OCSP check on the installer signature, and that's the OS, not OpenVoice.
  • If your laptop's strict egress firewall blocks Hugging Face (some corporate networks do), Tiny still works. You don't get stuck.

The 75 MB add to the installer is real. On a fiber connection it costs ~3 seconds of extra download time. On a 10 Mbps cellular tether it costs ~60 seconds. We think that's a fair price for first-run-without-network.

Why Tiny is enough to bootstrap

Whisper Tiny is the smallest of OpenAI's released Whisper variants - 39 million parameters, ~75 MB at q5_1 quantization. It's an order of magnitude smaller than Small (244M params, ~480 MB), two orders smaller than Large (1.5B params, ~3 GB).

Conventional wisdom says Tiny is for "low-resource scenarios" - Raspberry Pis, embedded devices, demos. But conventional wisdom underrates it for two reasons:

  1. Tiny is actually good at short utterances. The classic Whisper accuracy curve flattens out faster than you'd expect for utterances under ~15 seconds. The vast majority of dictation events - a Slack reply, an email subject line, a code comment, a calendar event title - are short utterances. Tiny gets them right at a rate that's surprisingly close to Small.
  2. Tiny runs in well under 100ms on a modern laptop. Latency is part of the dictation experience. Even when Small would be more accurate by a percentage point, Tiny's "instant" feel often wins. Users say "it feels faster" because it is faster.

We benchmarked this with internal users for two weeks. Half were on Tiny, half on Small, neither knew which. Tiny lost on long-form dictation (essays, meeting notes longer than a paragraph), about as expected. Tiny tied or won on short dictation. The takeaway: Tiny is not a placeholder; it's a legitimate everyday model for short utterances. We bundle it because it's actually usable, not because it's just a stopgap.

When you outgrow Tiny

The auto-provisioner, the first time you launch OpenVoice, looks at your hardware and recommends an upgrade if it thinks you'd benefit. The recommendation is roughly:

  • Recent laptop with 8 GB+ RAM, no dedicated GPU: stay on Tiny, or upgrade to Small for slightly better long-form.
  • Apple Silicon Mac (M1 or later): upgrade to Small - Metal makes it nearly as fast as Tiny.
  • Windows laptop with dedicated GPU (recent NVIDIA or AMD): upgrade to Small or Medium - Vulkan acceleration handles it.
  • Workstation / gaming desktop with 16 GB+ RAM and a recent GPU: consider Medium or Large.

The upgrade is a download - that's the unavoidable bit. Small is ~480 MB, Medium is ~1.5 GB, Large is ~3 GB. Bundling any of those into the installer would be antisocial. But by the time you trigger that download, you've already used OpenVoice, you've opted in, and the privacy hole is a one-time event rather than baked into the install path.

What the auto-provisioner is doing under the hood

The auto-provisioner lives in src-tauri/src/managers/auto_provisioner.rs. On first launch it:

  1. Runs system_probe::profile() to collect CPU model, total RAM, dedicated-GPU presence, GPU vendor (NVIDIA/AMD/Apple/Intel), and (on supported platforms) NPU presence.
  2. Passes the profile to backend_resolver::classify_tier(), which maps the profile to one of nine tiers (A through H, plus the fallback Tier-Tiny tier).
  3. Reads models_manifest.json to find the recommended Whisper variant for the resolved tier.
  4. Compares the recommendation against what's already on disk (Tiny is always there). If the recommendation is the same, no download. If higher, prompt the user to upgrade.

All of that decision logic runs locally. The only network traffic happens when the user accepts a recommendation that requires a larger model - and that traffic is a single HTTPS GET to the model CDN, transparent and visible.

What this means in practice

For everyday users: none of this matters in the immediate sense. You install OpenVoice, you dictate. The privacy story is a "what we don't do" feature: we don't make a network call before you've consented to one.

For compliance-sensitive users: this matters a lot. You can run the installer on a machine with strict egress rules, on a machine that's air-gapped, on a machine inside a SCIF, and dictation works. The air-gapped install post documents the path for environments where this is the explicit requirement.

For users on metered connections: Tiny means you can install and use OpenVoice on a cellular hotspot, on a train, on a flight Wi-Fi plan with a 100 MB cap. You're not blocked by a 480 MB Small download.

The principle

"Local-first" is a structural claim, not a marketing claim. An app that says "your audio stays on your device" but talks to three CDNs and a telemetry endpoint on first launch is making a claim that's technically true but spiritually false.

We're trying to make the structural claim and the marketing claim line up exactly. Bundling Tiny is the smallest, most obvious step toward that.
