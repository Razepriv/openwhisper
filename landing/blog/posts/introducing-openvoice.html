Introducing OpenVoice: voice dictation that stays on your machine

Wispr-Flow-class dictation for Mac, Windows, and Linux. Free, open-source, runs entirely on your hardware. Here's what shipped in v0.2.0 and why we built it.

By the OpenVoice maintainers June 11, 2026 7 min read
TL;DR
  • OpenVoice is a free, open-source desktop app for Mac, Windows, and Linux. Hold a hotkey, speak, release - the text is transcribed locally and pasted into whatever app you were just typing in.
  • No cloud, no API keys, no subscription. The Rust backend's URL allowlist makes it architecturally impossible to send audio to a third-party server.
  • Whisper Tiny ships in the installer. Larger models download on demand. Auto-provisioning picks the right one for your hardware on first run.
  • v0.2.0 is out today. Download below.

Voice dictation is one of those features that everyone has feelings about and almost nobody actually uses. Apple Dictation is built into every Mac and gets opened roughly twice a year. Windows Speech Recognition has been shipping since Vista and is somehow even less loved. Then a small SaaS called Wispr Flow turned up, charged twelve dollars a month, and lit the whole space up - because for the first time in a long time, dictation actually felt fast, accurate, and worth a hotkey.

The catch with Wispr is the same catch as with every SaaS-shaped product: your microphone is streaming to someone else's servers. That's fine for most users. It's not fine for legal pros, journalists, healthcare workers, anyone subject to GDPR, anyone working on unannounced product, or anyone who simply doesn't want a transcript of every Slack reply they dictated this year sitting in an account they don't control.

OpenVoice is the answer for those users. It does the same job - hold a hotkey, speak, release, watch the text appear - but the audio never leaves your machine.

What we built

OpenVoice is a fork of cjpais/Handy (MIT-licensed), extended with the Wispr-parity features that production users kept asking for: an auto-provisioning model picker, a 4-tier Auto Cleanup pipeline that runs a local LLM on top of the Whisper output, Command Mode for highlight-then-rewrite, a Personal Dictionary for jargon, and a Vibe Coding mode that detects when you're in an IDE or coding-agent terminal and stops capitalizing your variable names.

Underneath, the stack is deliberately boring:

  • Tauri 2.x for the app shell - Rust backend, React/TypeScript frontend, a real native installer on every platform.
  • whisper.cpp for transcription, with Vulkan on Windows, Metal on Mac, CUDA on Linux when available. The same library powers a dozen other local-Whisper apps, including MacWhisper.
  • llama.cpp as a sidecar process for the optional LLM cleanup pass.
  • Silero VAD for voice activity detection - the thing that decides when you've actually stopped talking and the transcript can be finalized.
  • cpal + rdev for cross-platform audio and global hotkeys.

All of it ships in the installer. The first time you run OpenVoice, it probes your hardware, picks the right Whisper model for your tier (we run through nine of them - see the auto-provisioner post for the matrix), downloads it once, and never asks again. From the second launch onward you're roughly two clicks from your first dictated sentence.

Why "100% local" is a feature, not a constraint

We get asked, fairly often, whether being local-first is actually worth it. Cloud STT models are huge. Cloud STT models keep getting better. Wispr Flow's accuracy is - and let's be honest about this - slightly ahead of Whisper Small on certain accents. So why are we doing this?

Three reasons, in order of how often they come up in user emails:

  1. Privacy that's enforceable, not promised. Plenty of dictation apps say they don't keep your audio. OpenVoice's Rust backend has an actual URL allowlist baked into llm_client::validate_local_url: 127.0.0.1, ::1, localhost, plus the OS-vendor on-device endpoints (apple-intelligence://, phi-silica://). Anything else returns an error from the validator before a request ever goes out. You can run a packet capture and watch nothing happen. It's the difference between "trust us" and "we removed the option."
  2. Zero subscription, forever. Wispr is $12-15/month. Dragon is $700 up front and Windows-only. Apple Dictation is free but tightly limited and doesn't work the way most modern users want - no LLM cleanup, no Command Mode, no Vibe Coding. OpenVoice is $0 and stays that way.
  3. Offline by default. No Wi-Fi, no degraded mode. The same hotkey works on the plane, in the basement, on a corporate laptop with strict egress rules. We've had users install OpenVoice on machines that have never seen the internet (see the air-gapped install post).

What's in v0.2.0

v0.2.0 is what we consider the "first version we'd recommend to a friend." Major surfaces:

  • Auto-provisioner. First-run probes your CPU/GPU/RAM, classifies you into one of nine hardware tiers, picks the matching Whisper variant (Tiny - Small - Medium - Large depending on tier), and downloads it. You can override - pick a smaller model for battery, a larger model for accuracy - but you don't have to.
  • Auto Cleanup (4 tiers). Whisper transcripts have characteristic quirks: repeated words, occasional missing punctuation, the word "uh" when you meant nothing of the sort. Auto Cleanup runs the raw transcript through a local LLM with a tightly-scoped rewrite prompt. Tiers run from Off (raw Whisper) to Light (punctuation + capitalization only) to Medium (tighten obvious filler) to High (full polish). Default is Medium.
  • Command Mode. Highlight any text in any app, hold the Command-Mode hotkey, speak instructions ("rewrite this to sound less corporate", "convert to bullets", "translate to formal French"), release. The selection is rewritten in place by the local LLM. This is the Wispr feature people miss most when they leave Wispr.
  • Personal Dictionary. Type your jargon - product names, internal acronyms, your CTO's hard-to-spell surname - and Whisper biases toward those tokens via the initial-prompt mechanism. Star a term to give it extra weight. See the Personal Dictionary post for the details.
  • Vibe Coding. When the focused app is Cursor, VS Code, Claude Code, Codex, Windsurf, Zed, JetBrains, or one of about twenty other coding tools, OpenVoice switches profiles: it stops capitalizing the start of fragments (because you're probably mid-identifier), it backtick-wraps things that look like code, and it converts spoken file paths into @-tags the way coding agents expect.
  • Transforms. Bind a hotkey to a custom LLM-rewrite prompt. Have a "format as a meeting note" transform, a "code-comment generator" transform, a "convert to bullets" transform. Hotkey runs them on whatever you just dictated. Five recipes are included to get you started.
  • Snippets. Speak a trigger phrase, get the full text. "@em" expands to your email signature. "@addr" expands to your business address. The simple thing, done well.
  • Cancel hotkey. Phone rings while you're dictating? Press the cancel hotkey, the recording stops, nothing gets pasted. The new widget exposes a clickable cancel button during recording, transcribing, and processing states.

How to get it

Direct downloads from the landing page - no GitHub Releases redirect, no account required, no waiting for a CDN you don't trust:

  • Windows (x64) - MSI and NSIS installers, ~85 MB after the Tiny model.
  • macOS (Apple Silicon + Intel universal) - DMG, ~80 MB.
  • Linux - .deb, .rpm, AppImage. Wayland support is partial; XWayland works.
